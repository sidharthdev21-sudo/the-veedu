## How we researched it  (0:20)

Here's how we researched it.
We started from Aalayam's brief: co-ord sets and sarees for women aged twenty to forty, at about one thousand to seventeen hundred rupees, sold online first.
Then we searched marketplaces like Nykaa, Flipkart and Amazon, brand websites and news profiles, and recorded real prices from product listings.
Every point is labelled: a verified fact, something we observed, our interpretation, a hypothesis, or a recommendation.

## The four levels of competition  (0:48)

First, a key distinction. Direct competitors sell a similar product, to the same customer, at a similar price. Indirect competitors meet the same need in a different way.
Level one is brand competition: Libas, Janasya, Juniper, Pink Fort and unbranded marketplace co-ords, plus Suta for sarees.
Level two is the wider industry: kurta-set and craft brands like Fabindia, comfort labels like Mulmul, and stores like Westside and Zudio.
Level three is a different product form: custom stitching, tailor-made outfits, or buying dress material.
And level four is the basic need itself: a local boutique, renting, buying second-hand, or simply wearing what's already in the wardrobe.

## The competitor matrix  (1:29)

Next, we built a competitor matrix.
Libas, Janasya, Juniper and Pink Fort all had co-ord listings between about eleven hundred and eighteen hundred rupees, and unbranded sets sold for around five hundred.
Nearly every listing showed a big discount, from twenty-six to sixty-nine percent off the printed price.
Some brands we couldn't price at all, so we left them off the maps rather than guess.

## Perceptual map: price and design  (1:52)

Then we drew our first perceptual map: price from affordable to premium, and design from basic to design-led.
The middle of the map is crowded: discount-led co-ords between one and two thousand rupees. Design-led brands like Suta sit well above that price.
Aalayam's stated price puts it right in the crowd. To stand out, it would need a much stronger design signature.
This map is a hypothesis, placed from prices and listings, not from customer opinion.

## Perceptual map: reach and comfort  (2:20)

Price and design hide something important: comfort, which is Aalayam's core promise. So we drew a second map.
Mass market to niche, and basic everyday wear to comfort-led lifestyle wear.
The brands that own comfort, Mulmul and Suta, are premium. The brands in Aalayam's price band compete on reach and print.
That leaves a possible gap: comfort-led clothing with a distinct identity, at an accessible price.

## Insights and positioning  (2:44)

So where does this leave Aalayam? Today, it competes in the most crowded band. Its strongest direct threats are mass brands with marketplace reach, while tailors and boutiques pull customers indirectly.
Our draft positioning: for women who want everyday clothes that feel as good as they look, Aalayam offers comfortable co-ords and sarees, rooted in Kerala, at honest prices, showing exactly what you're buying.

## Recommendations  (3:08)

We suggest four moves to test. These are recommendations, not things Aalayam does today.
Make comfort provable with fabric and fit details on every product. Keep one honest price instead of inflated discounts. Build a recognisable Kerala-rooted design signature. And offer fit help to compete with tailoring.

## Next steps  (3:27)

Before any of this is final, we need to validate it: a customer perception survey, interviews, and Aalayam's own price list.
PESTEL showed the environment. Five Forces showed the industry. And now we know who Aalayam competes with, and where it could stand apart.
