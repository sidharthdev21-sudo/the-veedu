# Voiceover script

Voice: Kokoro (af_heart, US English), speed 1.15. Total length 7:16.

## From strategy to reality  (0:00)

Six episodes of research pointed to one idea: comfortable co-ords that fit, at an honest price. But research only tells you what might work.
So Aalayam moved from studying the market to testing the business.
This episode documents that move: what we did, what it showed, and what it still can't tell us.
We started with an audit of what's real. The Shopify store is live, with thirteen products. The promised size range is L to triple XL. Instagram content and a test campaign have run. A pop-up was promoted in Kakkanad. And the team travelled across India to meet suppliers.
But Instagram insights, store analytics and supplier records aren't available to us, and the owner's notes mark every key metric as still to confirm. So we analysed what's public, the store itself, and built the system for the rest.

## Testing the audience  (0:47)

First, the audience. Aalayam created social pages and published content, with messages like feel the comfort, flaunt the style.
The goal wasn't likes. It was to learn which ideas make people stop, save, ask a question, or visit the store.
So we group content into experiments: product, styling, comfort, fit and size, brand story, and events.
And we compare groups with fair denominators: engagements, saves, enquiries and link clicks, each divided by reach.
Instagram's public page shows nothing without a login, and the first campaign was recorded as good results, without numbers. Good results can't guide a decision. Logged results can.

## The digital store  (1:26)

Next, the store, treated as a digital product. We read its public catalogue on the twenty-eighth of November.
Thirteen products are live: nine co-ord sets, an Anarkali set, an Onam saree, a set mundu and an Ajrakh skirt, all listed between late July and late August.
Co-ords are priced from one thousand to seventeen hundred rupees, with a median of twelve hundred. And no product shows an inflated M R P. Episode 6's honest price is already in practice.
Eleven of thirteen products follow the SKU format. Two Onam listings have no SKU or product type. Small gaps, but they break reporting later.

## The store journey  (2:04)

The customer journey has five stages: acquisition, discovery, evaluation, purchase, and retention, each with its own metric.
Evaluation is where clothing earns trust. The store's size guide offers a WhatsApp fit check: send your measurements, and the team recommends a size. Episode 6's idea, already live.
But garment measurements appear on only one of thirteen product pages, and cotton is named on six. That's the quickest improvement available.
Policies promise delivery in five to seven days and thirty-day returns. But the announcement bar mentions a thirty percent discount no price shows, and international shipping the owner said wasn't live. Both need confirming.
And low sales aren't proof of failure without traffic. At one percent conversion, a store sees zero orders in a hundred sessions more than a third of the time, by luck alone.

## The sourcing journey  (2:54)

Then came the hardest test: could Aalayam source the right garments on viable terms? The team travelled across India to find ready-made suppliers.
The catalogue already carries a sourcing signal. The promise is L to triple XL, but only two of nine co-ords are listed in all four of those sizes, and five in a single size. That often means small ready-made lots, but only the owner can confirm it.
Finding a supplier isn't validating one. We track seven stages: identified, contacted, information collected, sample received, evaluated, selected, and order placed.
Each supplier is scored on ten weighted criteria. And because Aalayam sells L to triple XL, size coverage is a gate: a supplier who can't make those sizes is rejected, however cheap.
In this illustrative example, the cheaper supplier fails that gate. Real quotes from the trip haven't been recorded, so no real supplier is scored.

## Unit economics  (3:50)

Sourcing only works if the numbers work. We start from a real price, the median co-ord at twelve hundred rupees. Every cost after that is illustrative, not Aalayam's actual cost.
Five percent GST leaves about eleven hundred and forty rupees. Subtract an illustrative six hundred rupee garment, packaging, shipping, payment fees and a returns allowance, and about a third remains.
The useful output is the limit: at this price, a garment must cost under roughly five hundred and seventy rupees to keep a thirty-five percent margin.
And minimum orders tie up cash. Thirty units per style, across four sizes, is stock that must sell before the next order.

## Connecting the business  (4:30)

These activities only become a business when they're connected.
Audience insight shapes a product decision. Supplier feasibility tests it. The store presents it. The audience responds, and that becomes the next insight.
One data model links them: content, enquiries, store sessions, products and suppliers, joined by content IDs, UTM links and the SKU.
Without that loop, a brand designs without demand, sources without margins, and launches without learning.

## Data strategy and dashboard  (4:58)

The data strategy is deliberately simple. Shopify for orders, Instagram insights for content, one shared sheet for everything else, and UTM links to trace a sale to its post.
The dashboard has four areas: audience, e-commerce, product and supply chain, and every tile shows its sample size, source and confidence.
Product tiles now hold real catalogue facts. Audience, sales and supplier tiles still say awaiting data, and that's exactly what the next thirty days should fix.
And no customer names, phone numbers or supplier contacts go into shared sheets, or this video.

## Product validation  (5:33)

To choose products, a validation scorecard weighs six criteria: customer relevance, audience interest, differentiation, supplier feasibility, unit economics, and operational risk.
The score maps to a decision, from reject to a small batch. Weak economics or an infeasible supplier stop a product, whatever its total.
In an illustrative run, the fit-confident co-ord earns samples, and a generic kurta set is rejected, matching Episode 5's advice to prune.

## What we learned  (6:01)

So what have we learned? Aalayam operates: thirteen live products, honest prices, a size guide with WhatsApp fit help, real content, an event, and a supplier search.
The biggest product gap is size coverage. The promise is L to triple XL, but only two of nine co-ords deliver it in full. For a plus-size brand, that's the first thing to fix, at the supplier.
The biggest business gap is evidence. Experiments ran, and their results weren't recorded. Until they are, good results is a feeling, not a finding.
Still unknown: content results by theme, the store funnel, the pop-up outcome, supplier quotes and real margins.

## Next steps  (6:38)

The next ninety days. Days one to thirty: organise. Reconnect store analytics, log every post, record the pop-up, enter every supplier, and add garment measurements to all thirteen products.
Days thirty-one to sixty: test. Interview customers, request samples, run fit content with UTM links, and build real unit economics.
Days sixty-one to ninety: decide. Score each product, approve suppliers, then launch a small batch, iterate, or stop.
We moved from understanding the market to testing the business. The next step is to turn what we learned into a repeatable and commercially viable operating model.
