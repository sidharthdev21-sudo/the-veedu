# Voiceover script

Voice: Kokoro (af_heart, US English), speed 1.15. Total length 6:25.

## From strategy to reality  (0:00)

Over six episodes, we studied the market, the competition, customers and growth options. The strategy pointed to one idea: comfortable co-ords that fit, at an honest price.
But research only tells you what might work. So Aalayam moved from studying the market to testing the business.
This episode documents that move: what we did, what evidence it produced, and what it still can't tell us.
We started with an audit. The store is live on Shopify. The size range is L to triple XL. Instagram content and a test campaign have run. A pop-up was promoted in Kakkanad. And the team travelled across India to meet suppliers.
But the analytics exports and supplier records aren't in the project yet, and the owner's own notes mark every key metric as still to confirm. That gap is the first finding.

## Testing the audience  (0:47)

First, the audience. Aalayam created social pages and published content, with messages like feel the comfort, flaunt the style.
The goal wasn't likes. It was to learn which ideas make people stop, save, ask a question, or visit the store.
So we group content into experiments: product, styling, fabric and comfort, fit and size, brand story, and events.
And we compare groups with fair denominators. Engagement rate is engagements divided by reach. Save rate, enquiry rate and link click rate work the same way.
The first campaign was recorded as good results, but without numbers. Good results can't guide a decision. Logged results can.

## The digital store  (1:25)

Next, the store, treated as a digital product. Shopify was chosen over Dukaan and WooCommerce, and every product follows one SKU format.
The customer journey has five stages: acquisition, discovery, evaluation, purchase, and retention. Each stage has its own metric, from sessions by source to add to cart, checkout and repeat purchase.
For clothing, evaluation is where trust is won. Garment measurements for every size, model size, fabric, daylight photos, and a clear return policy answer the doubts customers raised in Episodes 4 and 6.
And low sales aren't proof of failure without enough traffic. If the true conversion rate were one percent, a store would see zero orders in a hundred sessions more than a third of the time, by luck alone.

## The sourcing journey  (2:12)

Then came the hardest test: could Aalayam source the right garments on viable terms? The team travelled across India to find ready-made suppliers.
Finding a supplier isn't validating one. We track seven stages: identified, contacted, information collected, sample received, evaluated, selected, and order placed.
Each supplier is scored on ten weighted criteria. Quality carries the most weight. And because Aalayam sells L to triple XL, size availability is a gate: a supplier who can't grade those sizes is rejected, however cheap.
In this illustrative example, the cheaper supplier scores well overall, but fails the size gate. The quotes from the real trip haven't been recorded yet, so no real supplier is scored here.

## Unit economics  (2:57)

Sourcing only works if the numbers work. Here's a transparent cost model, with illustrative inputs, not Aalayam's actual costs.
At a fourteen hundred rupee price, five percent GST leaves about thirteen hundred and thirty rupees. Subtract the garment, packaging, shipping, payment fees and a returns allowance, and about forty percent remains.
The useful output is the limit: at this price, any quote above roughly six hundred and ninety rupees pushes the margin below thirty-five percent.
And minimum orders tie up cash. Thirty units per style, across four sizes, is stock that must sell before the next order.

## Connecting the business  (3:33)

These activities only become a business when they're connected.
Audience insight shapes a product decision. Supplier feasibility tests it. The store and content present it. The audience responds, and that response becomes the next insight.
One data model links them: content, enquiries, store sessions, products and suppliers, joined by content IDs, UTM links and the SKU.
Without that loop, a brand designs without demand, sources without margins, and launches without a way to learn.

## Data strategy and dashboard  (4:03)

The data strategy is deliberately simple. Shopify for orders and the funnel. Instagram insights for content. One shared sheet for content, enquiries, products and suppliers. And UTM links, so a sale can be traced back to the post behind it.
The dashboard has four areas: audience, e-commerce, product and supply chain. Every tile shows its value, its sample size, its source and its confidence.
Right now, the tiles say awaiting data. That's honest, and it's exactly what the next thirty days should fix.
And privacy matters: no customer names, phone numbers or supplier contacts in shared sheets, or in this video.

## Product validation  (4:40)

To choose products, we built a validation scorecard with six weighted criteria: customer relevance, audience interest, differentiation, supplier feasibility, unit economics, and operational risk.
The score maps to a decision: improve or reject, test the concept, request samples, or launch a small batch. Weak economics or an infeasible supplier stop a product, whatever its total.
In an illustrative run, the fit-confident co-ord scores high enough to request samples. A generic kurta set is rejected, which matches Episode 5's advice to prune.

## What we learned  (5:14)

So what have we learned so far? Aalayam can operate: a live store, real content, an event, and a supplier search.
The L to triple XL range sharpens the strategy. The fit problem from Episode 6 is specifically a plus-size problem, so supplier size coverage and fit proof become critical.
But the biggest learning is about evidence. Experiments ran, and their results weren't recorded. Until they are, good results is a feeling, not a finding.
Still unknown: content results by theme, the store funnel, the pop-up outcome, supplier quotes and real margins.

## Next steps  (5:48)

The next ninety days. Days one to thirty: organise. Reconnect Shopify, log every post, record the pop-up, and enter every supplier.
Days thirty-one to sixty: test. Interview customers, request samples from the top suppliers, run fit content with UTM links, and build real unit economics.
Days sixty-one to ninety: decide. Score each product, approve suppliers, and launch one small batch, iterate, or stop.
We moved from understanding the market to testing the business. The next step is to turn what we learned into a repeatable and commercially viable operating model.
