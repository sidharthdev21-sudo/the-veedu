| 2 | Testing the audience | Re-typeset recreations of the real Canva poster wording ("Feel the comfort. Flaunt the style.", "Cotton Comfy Wear", Olive Courtyard pop-up), with no customer images. Stamp: "Good results ≠ data". | First, the audience. Aalayam created social pages and published content, with messages like feel the comfort, flaunt the style. The goal wasn't likes. It was to learn which ideas make people stop, save, ask a question, or visit the store. So we group content into experiments: product, styling, fabric and comfort, fit and size, brand story, and events. And we compare groups with fair denominators. Engagement rate is engagements divided by reach. Save rate, enquiry rate and link click rate work the same way. The first campaign was recorded as good results, but without numbers. Good results can't guide a decision. Logged results can. |
| 3 | The digital store | A stylised (not real) store wireframe: Shopify chosen over Dukaan/WooCommerce; the SKU pattern AAL-TYPE-COLOR-SIZE building letter by letter. A probability bar: 0 orders in 100 sessions ≈ 37% · in 300 ≈ 5% (labelled: assumes 1% conversion). | Next, the store, treated as a digital product. Shopify was chosen over Dukaan and WooCommerce, and every product follows one SKU format. The customer journey has five stages: acquisition, discovery, evaluation, purchase, and retention. Each stage has its own metric, from sessions by source to add to cart, checkout and repeat purchase. For clothing, evaluation is where trust is won. Garment measurements for every size, model size, fabric, daylight photos, and a clear return policy answer the doubts customers raised in Episodes 4 and 6. And low sales aren't proof of failure without enough traffic. If the true conversion rate were one percent, a store would see zero orders in a hundred sessions more than a third of the time, by luck alone. |
| 4 | The sourcing journey | A stylised India map with a dotted route and generic pins labelled "locations to confirm" (no supplier names). The seven-stage supplier pipeline with counts "–". Banner: ILLUSTRATIVE, NOT ACTUAL DATA. | Then came the hardest test: could Aalayam source the right garments on viable terms? The team travelled across India to find ready-made suppliers. Finding a supplier isn't validating one. We track seven stages: identified, contacted, information collected, sample received, evaluated, selected, and order placed. Each supplier is scored on ten weighted criteria. Quality carries the most weight. And because Aalayam sells L to triple XL, size availability is a gate: a supplier who can't grade those sizes is rejected, however cheap. In this illustrative example, the cheaper supplier scores well overall, but fails the size gate. The quotes from the real trip haven't been recorded yet, so no real supplier is scored here. |
| 5 | Unit economics | A waterfall chart: ₹1,400 → GST → ₹1,333 → garment ₹600 → packaging ₹40 → shipping ₹80 → fees ₹28 → returns ₹32 → ₹553 (≈ 41%). A break-even slider: max purchase cost ≈ ₹690 for 35%. MOQ cash card: 30 units × ₹600 = ₹18,000 per style. Every number tagged ILLUSTRATIVE. | Sourcing only works if the numbers work. Here's a transparent cost model, with illustrative inputs, not Aalayam's actual costs. At a fourteen hundred rupee price, five percent GST leaves about thirteen hundred and thirty rupees. Subtract the garment, packaging, shipping, payment fees and a returns allowance, and about forty percent remains. The useful output is the limit: at this price, any quote above roughly six hundred and ninety rupees pushes the margin below thirty-five percent. And minimum orders tie up cash. Thirty units per style, across four sizes, is stock that must sell before the next order. |
| 6 | Connecting the business | An animated loop of seven nodes: Audience insight → Design decision → Supplier feasibility → Product selection → Store and content → Audience response → Learning. Then the data model diagram: Content · Enquiries · Shopify · Products · Suppliers, linked by content_id, UTM and SKU. Three "without the loop" warning chips. | These activities only become a business when they're connected. Audience insight shapes a product decision. Supplier feasibility tests it. The store and content present it. The audience responds, and that response becomes the next insight. One data model links them: content, enquiries, store sessions, products and suppliers, joined by content IDs, UTM links and the SKU. Without that loop, a brand designs without demand, sources without margins, and launches without a way to learn. |

Instagram insights for content. One shared sheet for content, enquiries, products and suppliers. And UTM links, so a sale can be traced back to the post behind it. | 9 | What we learned | Observation → Interpretation → Implication → Action cards for the main findings. The L–3XL insight card (linked to the Episode 6 plus-size fit study). "Good results is a feeling, not a finding." An "Still unknown" list with dashed outlines. | So what have we learned so far? Aalayam can operate: a live store, real content, an event, and a supplier search. The L to triple XL range sharpens the strategy. The fit problem from Episode 6 is specifically a plus-size problem, so supplier size coverage and fit proof become critical. But the biggest learning is about evidence. Experiments ran, and their results weren't recorded. Until they are, good results is a feeling, not a finding. Still unknown: content results by theme, the store funnel, the pop-up outcome, supplier quotes and real margins. |
| 10 | Next steps | A 90-day roadmap: Organise / Test / Decide with actions and KPI definitions. A final card with the closing statement, then the Aalayam wordmark, Episode 7. | The next ninety days. Days one to thirty: organise. Reconnect Shopify, log every post, record the pop-up, and enter every supplier. Days thirty-one to sixty: test. Interview customers, request samples from the top suppliers, run fit content with UTM links, and build real unit economics. Days sixty-one to ninety: decide. Score each product, approve suppliers, and launch one small batch, iterate, or stop. We moved from understanding the market to testing the business. The next step is to turn what we learned into a repeatable and commercially viable operating model. |
