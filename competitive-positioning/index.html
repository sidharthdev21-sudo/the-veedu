Who Aalayam competes with, and where it could stand apart

Four levels of competition and perceptual mapping for an online-first Indian women's clothing label: co-ord sets and sarees for women aged 20–40, at about ₹1,000–1,700.

Research compiled 24 November 2026 from web search results, marketplace listings and published articles. Prices are listings seen in search results on that date, not full catalogue ranges.

Summary

The short version

  • ObservedAalayam's price band (₹1,000–1,700) sits inside the busiest part of the co-ord market. Libas, Janasya, Juniper and Pink Fort all have co-ord listings between ₹1,087 and ₹1,819, and unbranded marketplace sets go as low as ₹498–549.
  • ObservedAlmost every listing in this band is sold "off MRP", with 26% to 69% discounts on the listings seen. Price is being used as the main lever.
  • InterpretationThe strongest direct threats are mass-market brands with marketplace reach (Libas, Janasya). The strongest indirect pull comes from tailoring (fit) and from premium "comfort" labels such as Mulmul and Suta, which own the comfort and ease story at higher prices.
  • HypothesisThe open space is not "cheaper" or "more fashionable". It is comfort you can verify, at an honest accessible price, with a recognisable Kerala-rooted design signature. No brand in this set combines all three, but this needs customer validation.
  • Important limitAalayam has no public listings that search could find, so its own position on the maps is a proposed position based on the brief. The maps are an initial hypothesis, not measured customer perception.
Framework

Direct and indirect competition

Direct competitors

Sell a similar product (co-ord sets or sarees) to the same customer (women 20–40), at a similar price, for the same occasion (everyday and smart-casual wear). The customer compares them side by side.

Indirect competitors

Meet the same underlying need in a different way: a different garment, a different way of getting clothes (tailoring, renting, second-hand), or a different use of the same money. Not every clothing brand is a direct competitor. We tested overlap in product, audience, price, need and positioning before classifying each one.

Deliverable 1

The four levels of competition, applied to Aalayam

1

Brand competition

Same product, same customer, similar price.

  • Indian co-ord and kurta-set brands at ₹1,000–2,000
  • Unbranded marketplace co-ord sellers
  • Instagram boutiques (not profiled individually here)
  • Modern saree labels for the saree line
2

Industry competition

Different clothing that meets the same wardrobe need.

  • Ethnic kurta and lehenga sets
  • Western and casual wear
  • Fusion and value fashion retail
  • Premium "comfort cotton" labels
3

Product-form competition

A different way of getting the same outfit.

  • Custom stitching and tailor-made outfits
  • Unstitched dress materials
  • Multi-purpose pieces mixed with what she owns
4

Generic need competition

Anything else that wins the same money or decision.

  • Local boutiques
  • Clothing rental (mainly occasion wear)
  • Second-hand and thrift
  • "I'll wear what I have"

Deliverables 5 and 6

Alternative map: reach × comfort

Initial hypothesis map: placements are analytical, not validated with customers

Why these axes

Aalayam's promise is comfort, and its route to market is a specific community (Kerala, then India and NRI customers). Mass versus niche captures whether a brand has a distinct identity or competes on reach. Basic versus comfort-led lifestyle captures whether comfort is the reason to buy, or just a fabric fact. Interpretation

What it shows

The brands that clearly own "comfort and ease" in this set are Mulmul (Supima softness) and Suta ("effortless" sarees), and both are premium-priced. Brands in Aalayam's price band are mass-market and sell on print and price. Observed Interpretation

Comfort-led lifestyle wear with a distinct identity at ₹1,000–1,700 looks under-served. This is the map that best fits Aalayam's brief. Hypothesis

Placement rationale: marketplace brands are placed mass-market because they rely on Flipkart, Myntra and Nykaa reach and deep discounts, and lower on comfort because their titles lead with print and occasion rather than feel. Suta is niche and comfort-led from its stated aim of an "effortless" everyday saree and its founder-modelled, homegrown voice. Mulmul is niche and comfort-led from its fabric-first Supima cotton claims. Every placement is relative and needs customer testing.

Deliverable 7

Strategic insights

1. Where is Aalayam competing today?

On its stated price and products, in Level 1 brand competition for co-ord sets at ₹1,000–2,000, the most crowded and discount-driven part of the market. Its saree line meets Suta at a lower price. Interpretation

2. Which competitors pose the greatest direct threat?

Libas and Janasya: same band, same occasion, marketplace reach and heavy discounting. Unbranded marketplace sets at around ₹500 pull the shopper's price expectation down. Interpretation

3. Which indirect alternatives influence customer choices?

Tailoring and custom stitching (better fit, personal choice), local boutiques (try-on and trust, likely strong in Kerala), and premium comfort labels that set the standard for "how comfortable clothes should feel". Rental matters little for everyday wear. Interpretation Hypothesis

4. Where is the market overcrowded?

Printed cotton and blended co-ord sets at ₹1,000–2,000, sold with inflated MRPs and 26–69% "off" tags on marketplaces. Observed

5. What positioning gaps could exist?

(a) Comfort you can verify (fabric, feel and fit stated honestly) at an accessible price. (b) A recognisable Kerala-rooted everyday design language: search surfaced no scaled Kerala D2C co-ord brand, though Instagram boutiques were not scanned. (c) Honest pricing without inflated MRPs. (d) Co-ords and sarees as one wardrobe for the same customer. Hypothesis

6. How could Aalayam differentiate itself?

By competing on proof of comfort and a distinct identity rather than on discounts: show the fabric and fit, keep one fair price, and give the collection a design signature people recognise. See the recommendations below. Recommendation

Deliverable 9

Strategic recommendations

Four moves a small brand could test, each with a way to measure it. All are recommendations, not current capabilities.

1. Make comfort provable on every product page

The move
State fabric composition, how it feels, garment measurements, the model's size and a plain "fits true to size / size down" note for every co-ord and saree.
Gap it uses
Reviews in this band complain about wide, inconsistent sizing and light fabric Observed. Marketplace titles lead with print, not feel.
How to test
Track size-related returns and exchanges, and product-page conversion, before and after.
Confidence
Medium. The pain point is observed; the payoff for Aalayam is our judgement.

2. Keep one honest price instead of inflated MRPs

The move
Show a single fair price, with the reason it's fair (fabric, making). Use occasional real offers rather than permanent "60% off".
Gap it uses
Every listing seen in the band was 26–69% off a higher MRP Observed. It also continues the Five Forces finding on transparent pricing for buyer power.
How to test
Run an A/B test or a split campaign of "one honest price" messaging against discount messaging; compare conversion and repeat purchase.
Confidence
Low to medium. Indian shoppers respond strongly to discounts, so this must be tested.

3. Build a recognisable Kerala-rooted design signature

The move
Carry one consistent design language across co-ords and sarees, for example handloom-inspired borders and palettes in everyday cuts, so a piece is recognisable as Aalayam.
Gap it uses
No scaled Kerala D2C co-ord brand surfaced in search Observed; Mass brands in the band compete on generic prints Interpretation. It also supports the planned NRI expansion.
How to test
Show customers unbranded photos of Aalayam and competitor pieces and ask which brand made each; track recognition and preference.
Confidence
Low to medium until Instagram boutiques in Kerala are scanned and customers are asked.

4. Take on tailoring with fit help

The move
Offer pre-purchase size help on WhatsApp or chat, and consider simple alteration allowances (extra seam margin) on bestsellers.
Gap it uses
Tailoring wins on fit, and doorstep tailoring services promise lifetime alterations Verified fact. Ready-made brands in the band rarely address fit directly Interpretation.
How to test
Track how many chats turn into orders, and returns on orders that used size help.
Confidence
Low. A hypothesis worth a small pilot.
