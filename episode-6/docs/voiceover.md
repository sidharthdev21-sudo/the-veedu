# Voiceover script

Voice: Kokoro (af_heart, US English), speed 1.15. Total length 5:46.

## Recap  (0:00)

In Episode 5, three frameworks agreed: comfort co-ords are Aalayam's best bet, sarees should stay a focused Kerala line, and the business needs evidence before it scales.
That told us where to focus. Now the question is how to grow.

## The growth challenge  (0:14)

Growth isn't simply more products or more markets. For a small brand, every new direction spreads the same team and budget thinner.
And in the thousand to two thousand rupee band, rivals sell similar co-ords at twenty-six to sixty-nine percent off M R P.
So how can Aalayam grow, and stand out, without competing only on price? We used three tools: the Ansoff Matrix, Blue Ocean Strategy, and the ERRC grid.

## Customer pain points  (0:39)

First, back to the customer. These are real pain points from public reviews and research, about other brands' products, not Aalayam's.
Fit comes first. Reviewers say trousers run small, and some sets skip the smallest sizes. A study of four hundred plus-size Indian women found poor fit at ten of twelve body points.
Second, sets don't match bodies. Tops and bottoms often need different sizes, and trouser lengths suit only shorter women.
Third, it looks different at home: colours that don't match the photos, thin fabric, and colour that bleeds in the wash.
The result is a familiar journey. Doubt the size, order two, return one, or pay a tailor. It costs the customer time, and the brand money.

## Customer interviews  (1:20)

Reviews tell us what goes wrong. Interviews tell us why. So we designed a programme: fifteen to twenty women aged twenty-four to thirty-eight who bought a co-ord online recently, including a few who still prefer a tailor.
Each question tests a hypothesis. Tell me about the last outfit you returned. Were your top and bottom the same size? What makes you trust a size? When do you go to a tailor instead?
The founder's earlier conversations point the same way, but those notes haven't been analysed yet. So interview findings here are a plan to test, not results.

## Ansoff Matrix  (1:53)

The Ansoff Matrix crosses products with markets. We scored thirteen ideas on attractiveness and feasibility.
Market penetration: fit-transparent pages and a WhatsApp review loop score high. They're cheap and fix known doubts. Deeper discounts? Avoid.
Market development: Malayali women in Bengaluru, Chennai and Mumbai come next. NRI shipping is risky, so it stays a waitlist.
Product development: the top idea is a fit-confident comfort co-ord, with top and bottom in separate sizes, and a Kerala design detail.
Diversification would spread a small team too thin. Not now.
So the primary direction is product development. Penetration ideas score as high, but anyone can copy a size chart. They become the enablers.

## Competitive landscape  (2:38)

Now look at how competitors actually compete.
Mass brands and unbranded sellers compete on price, discounts and range. Libas alone lists about three hundred co-ords. And almost everyone sells a set in one size for top and bottom.
Industry estimates put online fashion returns at twenty to thirty percent, driven mostly by size and fit.
Custom fit does exist, but mostly at premium prices, often with weeks of waiting and no returns.

## Blue Ocean opportunities  (3:04)

Blue Ocean Strategy asks: instead of beating rivals on the same factors, what value could Aalayam create that the market isn't offering?
We developed three potential concepts: fit-confident co-ords, Kerala everyday wear, and winning women who skip online clothing for a tailor.
None is a proven blue ocean yet. The first has the strongest evidence, so we take it forward, with the Kerala detail as its design layer.

## Strategy canvas  (3:29)

Here's the strategy canvas: eight factors customers weigh. The scores are research-based estimates, not customer ratings.
Unbranded sellers and mass brands follow the same shape: high on discounts and range, low on fit.
Premium brands solve fit, but at two to three times the price.
Aalayam's proposed curve breaks away: fewer discounts, a narrower range, and far more fit help, at a mid price. These are targets, not current performance.

## ERRC grid  (3:56)

The ERRC grid makes it concrete. Eliminate the inflated M R P, and the rule that a set comes in one size.
Reduce the range to six to eight core silhouettes, and rely less on marketplaces.
Raise fit information, with garment measurements, model size and daylight photos. Make one size exchange free.
Create split sizing with an adjustable waist and two lengths, plus a WhatsApp fit check that remembers each customer's sizes.
The promise shifts from comfortable, stylish and accessible, which everyone claims, to comfort co-ords that fit the first time.

## Strategic synthesis  (4:29)

Put together: Ansoff points to product development, Blue Ocean to fit confidence at a mid price, and ERRC defines the offer.
It builds on Episode 5: same hero category, same Kerala signature. What's new is that customer pain points move fit to the core.
The target customer: women aged twenty-four to thirty-eight, in Kerala and the metros, buying co-ords online, who've struggled with fit.
The risks: split sizing demand may be small, stock can fall out of balance, and exchanges cost money. So we test first.

## 90-day roadmap  (5:01)

Days one to thirty: validate. Run the interviews, survey how often sizes differ, audit five competitors, and record a Shopify baseline.
Days thirty-one to sixty: experiment. Launch a six-piece fit-confident capsule on pre-order, with fit-transparent pages and a WhatsApp fit check.
Days sixty-one to ninety: decide. Compare conversion, size returns and exchange costs with the baseline, then scale, iterate or stop.
No targets are invented. Every goal is set against Aalayam's own baseline.

## Closing  (5:33)

Aalayam doesn't need to be the cheapest, or the biggest. It can grow by solving the problem customers complain about most, and making fit its promise.
That's Episode 6 of the Aalayam business research series.
